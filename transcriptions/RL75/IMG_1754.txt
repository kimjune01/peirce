                    103 Irving St., Cambridge, Mass.,
                         March 26, 1902.

D. C. Gilman, LL.D.
        President, Carnegie Institution.

Dear Mr. Gilman:

        I have been intending for some time to write to you
regarding the work of Mr. Charles S. Peirce, the Logician, whose
character as an investigator, and whose need of assistance in
his scientific work, we discussed at our last interview. Since
I saw you I have had the opportunity to read two chapters of a
projected, but still incomplete work of Mr. Peirce on Logic. I
am able to give, in consequence, a very much more decided state-
ment than was possible when I saw you, regarding the value and
prospects of Mr. Peirce's enterprise.
        Mr. Charles Peirce has now been for many years the prin-
cipal representative in this country of a type of investigation
in Logic which seems to me, as a student of the subject, to be
of very great importance. The general nature of this importance
I can best indicate by saying that the study of the methods of
science and the comparative study of the types of concepts which
have been developed in the various sciences, seem to be at pres-
ent matters of great theoretical and practical significance, and
Mr. Peirce's methods of work seem to me to promise very great
results for both these types of study. What Mr. Peirce has al-
