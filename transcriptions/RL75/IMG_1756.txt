        As to Mr. Peirce's need of assistance in order to com-
plete the proposed work, there seems to be no doubt.  Here of
course plainness of speech is necessary.  Mr. Peirce is a worker
whose personal eccentricities are not unknown to you.  He has never
been able to fit into the official plans of other men for any
great length of time.  He has not been either the most frugal or
the most provident of scholars.  He is now obliged to earn a liv-
ing by various literary and other tasks that do not contribute to
science.  He lives more or less from hand to mouth, and probably
has no savings.  He is past sixty years of age.  While he seems
still capable of rapid and skillful production, his powers cannot
indefinitely continue to respond to the calls made upon him.  Un-
less he is aided to complete what I regard as his great task, his
later results will be lost to the world.  What I have lately seen
of these results convinces me more than ever that they ought not
to be lost.

        I venture therefore to urge, most respectfully, but
with the ardor of a student of logic who much needs further
light, and who hopes to get it from Mr. Peirce,- to urge, I say,
that in due time his work be supported, if possible, in some
way, by the Carnegie Institution, as a sort of work for which, as
I understand from you, the funds of the Institution can properly
be used.
