Mr. Peirce is now sixty-three years of age.  He cannot therefore
long enjoy possession of the necessary strength and energy to give to the
world the results of his investigations.  On his death, if these remain
unpublished, the possibility of adding this contribution to learning will
have passed, and there will remain of his work in this field only some
more or less fragmentary records published and unpublished.  Such a mis-
chance would be a calamity to science and the loss of an opportunity of
adding to the scientific reputation of our country.

The science of logic is not of such general popularity as to permit
the publication of the results of the most profound investigations in its
domain in the ordinary manner, except by the favored few whose independent
means enable them to disregard the pecuniary loss involved in such an
undertaking, or which may be involved in it.

It would seem that here is a case of exactly the class which Mr.
Carnegie had prominently in view in founding the Institution which bears
his name.  Here is precisely "the exceptional man" in his department of
study, who seeks the means of preparing and publishing the magnum opus
which he alone is qualified to produce, and which will constitute an im-
portant contribution to learning; while the daily needs of bare existence
compel him, without such aid as a great institution can furnish, to devote
his thought and energy to labors commanding immediate cash returns to
defray the cost of living,- a perversion of valuable mental gifts from
their proper sphere of usefulness which it was clearly the design of
the founder to prevent.
