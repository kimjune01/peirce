                         Harvard University, March 21, 1902.


Dr. Daniel C. Gilman,

          Chairman of the Carnegie Institution,

               Washington.


Dear Sir:

     I understand that Mr. Charles Sanders Peirce of Milford (Pa) has

applied to your trustees for aid in the completion of his work on Logic.

     I wish to say emphatically that I can hardly conceive of a more appro-

priate case for help.  Although a man of genius in the purest sense of the word,

Peirce has proved unable (some malignant witch having cast a spell upon him in

his cradle, no doubt) to make more than the barest living, and now, as I under-

stand it, with this Magnum Opus on his mind, has no regularly remunerative sup-

port.  He is in the very front rank of American thinkers (I, for example, owe

more to his writings than to those of anyone but Royce) and his Logic when pub-

lished will unquestionably (in spite of certain probable obvious oddities) be

recognized all over the world as an epoch-making work.  Ignoring all personal

questions, and regarding only the interests of originality in Science, I urge

you emphatically to consider Mr. Peirce's case as one for favorable treatment.

                              Respectfully yours,

                                   (signed) Wm. James.
