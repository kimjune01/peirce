Mr. Peirce is now sixty-three years of age.  He cannot therefore long
enjoy possession of the necessary strength and energy to give to the world
the results of his investigations.  On his death, if these remain unpub-
lished, the possibility of adding this contribution to learning will have
passed; and there will remain of his work in this field only some more or
less fragmentary records published and unpublished.  Such a mischance
would be a calamity to science and the loss of an opportunity of adding
to the scientific reputation of our country.

The science of logic is not of such general popularity as to permit the
publication of the results of the most profound investigations in its do-
main in the ordinary manner, except by the fewest few whose independent
means enable them to disregard the pecuniary loss involved in such an un-
dertaking, or which may be involved in it.

It would seem that here is a case of exactly the class which Mr. Car-
negie had prominently in view in founding the institution which bears his
name.  Here is presently "the exceptional man" in his department of study,
who lacks the means of preparing and publishing the results upon which he
alone is qualified to produce, and which will constitute an important
contribution to learning; while the daily needs of bare existence compel
him, without such aid as a great institution can furnish, to devote his
thought and energy to labors consuming immediate cash returns to defray
the cost of living,- a perversion of valuable mental gifts from their
proper sphere of usefulness which is was clearly the design of the founder
to prevent.
